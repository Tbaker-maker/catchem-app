How our numbers are made

Every figure on Catch'em carries a chip — VERIFIED (measured from listings) or READ (our interpretation, always with a stated way to prove it wrong). This page is the receipts. Updated Sep 27, 3:19 AM PT.

The Catch'em Sealed Index

The five-year-old version 🍭
Imagine a shelf with 153 Pokémon boxes. Every morning we check every price, then squish them into one magic number. On day one we called it 100 — the starting line. Every day, each box answers one question: am I worth more or less than when we started? We average the answers. 100.6 means the shelf is worth a tiny bit more than at the start. Brand-new sets sit on the bench for their first three months — launch-week excitement is not market weather, so they join once the dust settles. And each box only competes against itself — the $2,900 box and the $99 box each get one vote, so no giant box can hog the number. Breadth just counts hands: today 42 boxes said "I went up," 4 said "I went down." Sunny day on the shelf.

Why there are two index numbers

The five-year-old version 🍭
Imagine the shelf again. In the main number, every box gets one vote — the tiny $8 pack and the $5,000 box shout equally loud. That is fair in one way and odd in another, because a cheap pack's price jumps around a lot more, so the cheap end does most of the shouting. So we also keep a second number where the expensive boxes get a bigger say. When the two numbers agree, the whole shelf is moving together. When they disagree, one end of the shelf is doing something the other end is not — and that is worth knowing.

The grown-up version: the headline index is equal-weight — each product's move counts once, regardless of price. Equal weighting is a deliberate choice, not an oversight, and it has a known consequence we would rather state than hide: measured on 2026-08-22, the cheapest quartile of products (median $20) accounted for 54% of the day's total movement while the priciest quartile accounted for 20%, because low-priced items move further in percentage terms. The value-weighted twin weights each product's move by what it was worth at its baseline, so a $5,000 box counts for more than a $10 pack. Both are published. One gives every product an equal vote. The other gives expensive products a bigger vote. A gap between them means the cheap end and the expensive end are moving apart.

Composition, stated plainly: as of writing, the index runs roughly 39% Scarlet & Violet, 31% Sword & Shield, 18% Mega Evolution, 11% Sun & Moon and 1% older — so it describes the modern sealed market more than the whole history of it. By product class it is close to even across elite trainer boxes, booster boxes, Pokémon Center elite trainer boxes, loose packs and bundles.

The grown-up version: equal-weight composite: every tracked sealed product's price today divided by its own first clean-history price, averaged, times 100. Baseline 100 = 2026-08-19, the first FULL day under our fixed-price listings methodology. The changeover day itself is excluded, and new sets wait 90 days before joining — launch-week excitement is not the market — the index measures market movement, never our own cleanup. (Standard practice: real indexes chain-link across method changes for exactly this reason.) Breadth counts how many products rose vs fell day-over-day. Composition changes don't distort the level because each product is measured against itself.

Prices

Single booster packs are priced from TCGplayer, not eBay. A sealed pack is a commodity — any copy is any copy — so the premium eBay earns on boxes by showing you the exact item has no justification here, and eBay pack asks measured 8-51% above TCGplayer on the same products. TCGplayer is where this class actually trades, so that is the price we show; the eBay asking median is kept alongside for comparison, never hidden. Everything else on the board is priced from eBay. Shipping is included in our eBay prices. Tax is never included, anywhere. Our TCGplayer figures are the exception and currently exclude shipping, because that is how the source reports them — which means an eBay-vs-TCGplayer comparison is not yet like-for-like, and we would rather say so than quietly estimate postage. All prices are natively in US dollars (eBay US marketplace, pinned in code). Choosing CAD in the app shows a converted estimate at the day’s rate, clearly labeled — the underlying data never changes currency. Sealed prices are eBay asking medians and they are DELIVERED TOTALS — item plus shipping — wherever a listing states a shipping cost. Some sellers list an item cheaply and recover the difference in postage, so a sticker price on its own is not what you would actually pay; comparing delivered totals is the only way to compare listings fairly. Where a listing states no shipping cost, that is a free-shipping listing — the seller has folded postage into the asking price, so the item price already is the delivered price. We never estimate postage. Beyond that: fixed-price listings only, scam-vocabulary filtered, bounded per product class, with a lowest-ask figure that ignores implausible outliers. We do not have sold-price data and we don't pretend to.

Demand (est.)

The five-year-old version 🍭
Imagine a toy-shop shelf. Every morning we count how many of each box are sitting on it. If yesterday there were 40 and today there are 31 — and no delivery truck came — kids have been grabbing them. That grabbing is Demand. We never get to peek at the cash register (nobody outside eBay does), so we watch the shelf instead. Shelf emptying fast = pressure rising. Shelf filling up = sellers piling in.

The grown-up version: Demand is the day-over-day change in a product's active listing count, read as inventory draining (demand-side) or building (supply-side). How to read it: a steady drain across several days matters far more than one day's dip; drain plus a rising ask median is the strongest read; a sudden jump usually means restock or seller pile-in, not collapse.

Why it wears a READ chip, not VERIFIED: the shelf can lie in small ways — sellers delist and relist, listings expire, a restock lands mid-count. We can measure the shelf perfectly and still only estimate the grabbing. That is why Demand is always paired with price and never published alone, and why the chip says READ: it is our interpretation, with the method shown. Active Listings, by contrast, are simply counted — that number is VERIFIED.

Supply Shifts

When a shelf count jumps or drains sharply (±15% with at least 20 listings) we flag it with cause candidates: supply up + price down = seller wave (a reprint hitting, reprint rumors, or a big holder exiting); shelves draining while asks rise = absorption (demand — or a whale sweeping). If our catalyst log already recorded a matching event, the flag says so. Candidates, never verdicts — the chip stays READ.

Marketplace gaps (not on the front page)

We demoted this one on purpose.
This compared our eBay price against the TCGplayer price for the same product. It used to help choose what we showed you. It no longer does, and here is the honest reason: the two numbers are not measured the same way and we cannot fix it. Our eBay figure is what you would actually pay delivered, postage included. The TCGplayer figure is an item price with shipping excluded — that is how the source reports it, and no shipping-inclusive TCGplayer figure is available to us at any subscription tier. So the gap always reads wider than the real difference, and worst on cheap products where postage is most of the cost. A number biased in a known direction by an unknowable amount should not decide what you read.

A genuine disagreement between two marketplaces is still worth knowing about. This comparison is not a headline, and it does not choose the Daily Three. We still compute it internally — if we ever obtain shipping-inclusive TCGplayer data, it can come back, and we will say so.

What the two numbers are: our eBay figure is what listings ask. The TCG figure is the TCGplayer Market Price — their own average of recent completed sales on the US marketplace, in US dollars. Those are different instruments before shipping even enters it: asks sit above completed sales, and eBay's photos of the exact item earn a real premium. A flat TCG line can simply mean no recent sales rather than a stale price. Tax is excluded on both sides and always will be — it depends on your state and the seller, so any figure we printed would be wrong for most readers.

Venue law: Sun & Moon and older sealed rarely trades on TCGplayer — that market lives on eBay, card shows, and collector groups. For those eras we publish eBay-native stats only and gate the cross-market comparison entirely.

Raw & Graded — same equation, different shelves

The Raw Chase Index and the Graded Index use the exact same magic-number trick as the Sealed Index — same starting line at 100, same one-product-one-vote rule — just pointed at different shelves: confirmed chase singles, and graded copies. One explanation covers all three. The Graded Index switches on when a licensed daily graded-price feed exists.

Grading Premium (a different instrument): graded sold price minus raw market minus the grading fee. On established sets a 9 usually runs negative — the grading tax; only the 10 pays. Fresh sets bend this until graded supply catches up. Exact figures publish on licensed surfaces.

Heat reads — the weather report

The four weathers of a Pokémon box 🍭 (plus a calm day)
🔥 Heating up: more people want the box than there are boxes — price climbing while the shelf empties.
❄️ Cooling off: fewer hands reaching — price sliding while boxes pile up.
😴 Quiet gathering: nobody's shouting, but the shelf keeps emptying — the quiet before a move.
📤 Selling into strength: price looks strong but sellers keep restocking the shelf into it — strength being sold.
⏸ A calm day: price wandering inside its usual room — no weather to report.

The grown-up version: Wyckoff-style states from two measured signals — weekly price change × listing-count flow (Demand est.). Dual-signal verdicts publish only after 8 clean history days per product; before that we publish nothing false.

Depth reads — how full is the shelf?

The five-year-old version 🍭
⏳ Still counting: we need three clean days before we say anything — honesty over speed.
📉 Shelf emptying: listings disappearing faster than they're replaced — scarcity forming.
📦 Shelf filling: more copies arriving than selling — restocking, or interest fading; context decides.

The grown-up version: a 3-day listing-count flow per product — Active Listings are measured, the flow verdict is Demand (est.). Verdicts unlock at 3 clean days each; ⏳ until then.

Print Watch — the closing window

The five-year-old version 🍭
Pokémon doesn't print a set forever — the printer runs for about two and a half years, then moves on. Print Watch is a countdown clock for every set still being printed: when it hits zero, no new boxes are ever made, and the shelf you see is the shelf there will ever be. The countdown is our estimate (the ⏳ number), not an announcement — Pokémon can surprise us and print more. "Tightening" sets already stopped printing AND their shelf is getting empty.

The grown-up version: a 30-month print-window model per set, crossed with live listing depth (supply tiers) and reprint-news signals. Estimates carry (est.) everywhere; a reprint announcement resets the clock and we say so in public.

The clean history

Every sparkline, Δ badge and mover compares committed daily history that began 2026-08-18 — the day the fixed-price listings methodology went live. Earlier rows were contaminated and are never charted. The lines are the same for every visitor, first visit included, and grow one point per morning run.

The fair range

On a product page, asks cluster between the lowest ask (cheapest clean listing) and the median. Offers under the lowest ask are reaching; asks past the median need a reason. That band is what Deal Check prints — and the value snapshots that settle raffle cash-outs come from the same numbers.

Per-pack & the sealed premium

Per-pack basis: TCGplayer market where mapped (packs are commodities — the photo premium has no justification in this class, RT-4b); eBay asks only as a flagged fallback while mappings complete.

Per-pack = ask median ÷ era-aware pack count (36 for booster boxes, 8–9 for trainer boxes, counts that vary aren't printed). The sealed premium compares that to the same set's loose-pack street price — positive means you pay extra to keep it sealed; negative is the bulk discount. A loose lane with under 10 listings is flagged thin, not hidden.

The Deal Zone

At a card show, both sides of the table are comparing cash against the online route — and the online route costs each of them differently. A buyer's true online cost is the delivered eBay total plus sales tax (est., default 7%). A seller's true online outcome is the ask minus marketplace fees (est., default eBay's 13.25% + $0.40). The gap between those two estimates is the Deal Zone. The labeled prices on a product page are different numbers: the lowest ask, the median, and today's high. Those three always match across the page. The fee and tax lines are estimates and are labeled as estimates.

The five-year-old version 🍭
Imagine you and a friend both know a toy costs $10 at the store — but getting it delivered really costs the paying side $11, and the store only hands the seller $9 of it. If you meet at the park, ANY price between $9 and $11 makes you both happier than the store would. That $2 stretch is the Deal Zone. We just do the math for every box, every morning.

Everything we track is public. Every sealed product is on the Board. A number whose universe is secret is a number you have to take on faith.

Where we have no second opinion

Some of what we publish can only be confirmed by one source, and we would rather say which than let you assume corroboration we do not have. The index level is our own measurement — no external body publishes a sealed Pokémon index to check us against, so it is internally consistent rather than independently verified. Pack prices come from TCGplayer through a single provider; if that provider is wrong, we are wrong the same way. The deal zone is built on published marketplace fee schedules, which change without notice — we recheck them, but between rechecks the figure ages.

None of that makes the numbers unreliable. It makes them single-sourced, which is a different and smaller claim than the word "verified" usually carries, and you should know which one you are reading.

House reads & falsifiers

Interpretations (reprint cycles, depth reads, the PSA-9 tax — on established sets, a 9 usually returns less than the raw card plus the grading fee; only the 10 pays) publish with the condition that would prove them wrong, and when a falsifier trips we amend in public. Being seen self-correcting is the point.
